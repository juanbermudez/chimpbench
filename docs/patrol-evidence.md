# Patrol evidence check (C6, Step 1)

Evidence check for the C6 patrol corrections pre-registered in [realism-design.md §5.3.1](realism-design.md) (P1–P5). It verifies or refutes seven gaps raised by a side review of the C6 patrol code. Checked on 29 September 2026. The code was read, not changed. The line numbers refer to the code on that date.

**How to read this.**

- **Access.** "FT" means the full text was read in this pass. "Abs" means the abstract only. "Idx" means only an indexed excerpt of the paper was visible. Anything not seen is marked **unverified**.
- **Relation.**
  - *Predictor:* a variable tested in a statistical model of the outcome. The design is still observational.
  - *Correlate:* an association without a model of the outcome, or a descriptive pattern.
  - *Cause:* the stimulus was experimentally manipulated.
  - No study below shows that anything causes patrols. Only the playback study manipulates anything, and it manipulates the stimulus heard.
- **Confidence.** High, moderate or low. It applies to the finding within its own scope, not to its transfer to the simulation.

## 2. The seven gaps: verdicts and contradictions with the C6 code

Each item gives the code location, what the evidence says, a verdict, a recommended change and a confidence. The pre-registered changes in realism-design.md §5.3.1 are referenced where they apply.

### Gap 1. Hearing strangers doubles the patrol hazard: **confirmed**

- **Code.**
  - [perception.ts](../src/sim/perception.ts) `rollImpulses` l. 241–242: `heard = world.time − x.heardAt < 24 ? 1 : 0`, then `h = … · (1 + P.patrolHeardBeta · heard)`.
  - [data/params.json](../data/params.json) sets `patrolHeardBeta` = 1, labelled "design".
- **Evidence.**
  - Mitani & Watts 2005 tested intruder pressure directly. On patrol days the last encounter was *longer* ago (28.9 vs 24.5 d; P = 0.33; logistic P = 0.43, e^b = 1.004). The subsample gave the same result (P = 0.61).
  - Oestrous females had no effect (P = 0.17).
  - The significant predictors were male party size (+17% odds per male, R² 0.29) and fruit. Fruit held only in the bivariate model (R² 0.05). It was not significant with party size in the model or in the subsample. The "72 patrols / 623 follow-days" figure is correct, but the regressions compare the 72 patrol days with 72 random non-patrol days, not with all 623.
- **Caveat.** The measure counts days since the last encounter, not a 24-h window. Encounters reached Ngogo parties only every few weeks, so the study cannot exclude a short-lived effect. It gives no support for one, and the sign runs the other way.
- **Keep the reaction.** The immediate reaction to heard strangers (V.APPROACH / V.HEARD in [candidates.ts](../src/sim/candidates.ts) l. 520–527) is a different behaviour. It is experimentally supported: parties with ≥ 3 males chorus and approach; smaller parties stay silent (Wilson et al. 2001).
- **Recommendation.** Adopt P1 as pre-registered: `patrolHeardBeta` 1 → 0. P1's pending tag becomes **[M]** (mitaniWatts2005: a null result for intruder pressure). Keep the V.APPROACH reaction.
- **Confidence:** moderate. This is a single-site null with 72 vs 72 days and a coarse measure.

### Gap 2. Fruit enters only through party energy: **partly confirmed**

- **Code.**
  - [perception.ts](../src/sim/perception.ts) l. 236–239: `E` = clamp((mean energy of the adult males in view − `patrolEnergyLow`) / (1 − `patrolEnergyLow`)), with `patrolEnergyLow` 0.4. A party at mean energy ≤ 0.4 therefore has a hazard of zero.
  - realism-design.md §5.3 attributes T-PAT-4's fruit effect to this term.
- **Evidence.**
  - The fruit effect is weak and not independent of male party size (Wald 1.58, P = 0.21 in the joint model; P = 0.20 in the subsample). Fruit and party size are correlated (P = 0.01).
  - Mitani & Watts note that low fruit did not appear to deter patrols. They suggest males intent on patrolling may draw others into large parties.
  - Watts et al. 2006: patrols and encounters cluster when males form large parties, often at large fruit crops.
  - Kanyawara: food, especially *Uvariopsis*, sets *when and where* encounters happen (Wilson et al. 2012).
  - Amsler 2010: patrols cost feeding and travel time.
  - The documented pathway is therefore fruit → male party size → patrol, plus the location of fruit → where contact happens. It is not "low party energy blocks patrols". The claim "fruit was a significant predictor" holds only in the bivariate analysis.
- **Recommendation.**
  - Do not add a direct fruit term.
  - Make `E` a soft multiplier with a floor, e.g. E ∈ [0.5, 1], instead of a gate. Label it design.
  - Add a non-circular check: daily maximum male party size should rise with community fruit availability. That is the mediator the field result depends on.
  - Record T-PAT-4's fruit effect as expected *through party size*, not through energy.
- **Confidence:** moderate for the finding; low for the functional form.

### Gap 3. Female participation fixed to Ngogo's pattern: **confirmed** (site difference). The lactation exclusion is **contradicted** at Taï

- **Code.**
  - [candidates.ts](../src/sim/candidates.ts) `patrolAndCalls`:
    - l. 637: continuing a patrol costs females −0.3.
    - l. 639: a female joins with score 0.2; a lactating female gets −1.
  - l. 525: females get −0.5 on V.APPROACH.
  - P3 moves these literals to the registry, with an override in `data/presets/tai-patrols.json` that leaves "lactating unchanged".
- **Evidence.**
  - Ngogo: females essentially absent (Watts & Mitani 2001).
  - Taï: adult females on 57% of patrols, 2.2 per patrol (Boesch & Boesch-Achermann 2000, N = 38, as cited by Watts & Mitani 2001; secondary). Taï females took part in 48% of active encounters, over 92% of which followed a patrol. A young infant had no clear effect. Maximal swelling raised participation; late gestation lowered it (Samuni et al. 2021).
  - Gombe: patrol parties held a median of 3 adult females (0–20) and 1 oestrous female (Massaro et al. 2022).
  - Kanyawara: intergroup aggression is described as almost exclusively male, and oestrous females reduce males' approach and southward travel (Wilson et al. 2012).
  - Ngogo is therefore the low end among three sites. Kibale's default is defensible.
- **Recommendation.**
  - Adopt P3 (site setting, Ngogo default). P3's pending tag becomes **[M]**.
  - Correct the Taï preset:
    - Do not exclude lactating females (`patrolLactatingJoin` = the female base, not −1).
    - Add a swelling bonus.
    - Add a late-gestation penalty.
    - Label all three as sourced from samuni2021 (encounter participation, not patrols alone).
  - A Gombe preset could use Massaro's composition as a held-out target.
- **Confidence:** moderate-to-high for the site difference; moderate for the infant and swelling effects.

### Gap 4. Routes use only border staleness: **confirmed**

- **Code.**
  - [territory.ts](../src/sim/territory.ts) `stalestSector` / `stalestOf` (l. 245–283) picks the neighbour-facing octant with the oldest visit by males to the periphery.
  - [execution.ts](../src/sim/execution.ts) `startPatrol` (l. 341–345) routes to it.
  - [perception.ts](../src/sim/perception.ts) l. 240 scales the hazard by its staleness `S`.
  - Nothing reads where contacts, losses or killings happened.
- **Evidence.**
  - **Killings:** 13 of 21 Ngogo killings (61.9%) happened in the NE, in an area of heavy patrol activity. The range expanded into that area in 2009, by 6.4 km² (22%) (Mitani et al. 2010; confirmed by Wood et al. 2025 and Langergraber et al. 2017). This claim is **confirmed**.
  - **Concentration:** patrols concentrated on the NE, E and SE boundaries. Sector 1 was visited 20 times in 11 months, at a median interval of 9 d and 13 times in 92 d. Sector 4 went 158 d without a patrol. The west, facing another very large community, was largely avoided (Watts & Mitani 2001).
  - **Returns to a hot spot:** a patrol returned two days later to where neighbours had been, and killed there (Watts et al. 2006).
  - **Kanyawara:** 63% of encounters were in the SE quadrant, tied to the location of food (Wilson et al. 2012).
  - **After the fission:** patrols shifted to the new boundary (Sandel & Watts 2021).
  - **Food anecdote:** at Ngogo, patrolling appeared to increase before seasonal fruit abundance near boundaries (Mitani & Watts 2005, discussion).
- **What this means.** A pure stalest-sector rule predicts rotation, with most attention on the most neglected edge. The field shows the opposite. Patrols repeat at short intervals on contested, food-rich edges facing weaker neighbours, and edges facing strong neighbours go months without one.
- **Recommendation.** P2's direction (route interest from contact memory) is supported, but its form is too weak to reproduce the pattern.
  - In `S × (1 + patrolContactW · min(1, C/dangerScale))` with a weight of 1, a never-visited cold sector (S → 1) beats a hot sector visited < 5 d ago (S < 0.5 at τ = 7 d). No sector can be neglected for months.
  - Let contact dominate. Two options:
    - an additive score, `w_s·S + w_c·C − w_l·L`, where L is the leader's loss weight in that sector (avoid edges where the community lost);
    - or `patrolContactW` ≥ 3 together with a shorter `patrolStaleTauDays`.
  - Optionally add a food term: fruit near the sector's edge.
  - A non-circular held-out: patrols per sector are uneven (e.g. the top sector gets ≥ 1/3 of patrols in a year), and at least one neighbour-facing sector goes ≥ 100 d unpatrolled.
  - The weights stay design. The pattern is **[M]** (wattsMitani2001, mitani2010, wilson2012).
- **Confidence:** moderate. The evidence is descriptive, from two Kibale sites.

### Gap 5. The danger map is community-global and wins leave no spatial trace: **partly confirmed**

- **Code.**
  - `s.danger[troopId]` in [territory.ts](../src/sim/territory.ts) (one grid per community) is written by `markDanger`. The callers are [conflict.ts](../src/sim/conflict.ts) (deaths, injuries), [execution.ts](../src/sim/execution.ts) l. 248 (flight from strangers) and [parties.ts](../src/sim/parties.ts) l. 164 (a patrol turns back).
  - Nothing is written when the community wins, chases or kills.
- **Evidence that wins leave a trace (confirmed).**
  - Killings concentrated where the community later expanded (Mitani et al. 2010).
  - Males returned to a contested spot and killed there (Watts et al. 2006).
  - Territory gain followed killings, with measured fitness gains afterwards: 15 births in the three years before vs 37 in the three years after, and infant mortality to age 3 fell from 41% to 8% (Wood et al. 2025).
- **Community-global vs individual (unverified).** No field study found tests whether spatial risk knowledge is individual or shared.
  - Individual differences in edge use exist: low-ranking males drop out on far trips (Wilson et al. 2012), and some males' presence raises periphery visits (Gilby et al. 2013).
  - Patrol partners are grooming and coalition partners (Watts & Mitani 2001).
  - This is consistent with P2's per-chimp memory shared by travelling together, but does not test it.
- **Recommendation.**
  - P2's "no separate wins map" is acceptable only if wins and kills write *contact* weight C at the site. P2 already writes C when "strangers seen in an encounter", which covers a chase. Add an explicit kill write: C += `dangerDeathW` at a kill by own males.
  - The avoidance term must read only the loss weight L, so the kill site attracts use rather than repelling it.
  - P2's per-chimp storage stays design.
- **Confidence:** moderate for "wins attract later use"; low for any mechanism.

### Gap 6. Nothing physiological drives patrol readiness: **partly confirmed** (the source claim is verified; the implied mechanism is not)

- **Code.** No readiness state. §5.3.1 defers one until Samuni et al. 2017 is verified.
- **Evidence.**
  - Verified: urinary oxytocin rose *before* and *during* Taï patrols in both sexes (details in the table).
  - The anticipation result rests on one group, 6 individuals and 14 samples. It was measured after multi-partner grooming, which often precedes patrols at Taï.
  - The authors say the direction is unresolved. It is a correlate, in *P. t. verus*.
  - The testosterone rise at Ngogo (Sobolewski et al. 2012) is **unverified** (indexed excerpt only).
  - No study shows that a hormonal state predicts *whether* a patrol starts.
- **Recommendation.**
  - Close the deferred item. The evidence does not require a readiness state, and none should be added.
  - Optional, site-specific, low confidence: at Taï, multi-male grooming shortly before could raise the hazard, as an observable precursor.
  - The cohesion result (fewer fissions during patrols) supports P4a's single file and the stay-with-the-leader rule. Massaro et al. 2022 agrees: males stayed a median 96% of the patrol.
- **Confidence:** moderate (the finding); low (any mechanism).

### Gap 7. Who initiates a patrol is weakly evidenced: **confirmed**. The alpha bonus is **contradicted**

- **Code.**
  - Every eligible adult male rolls the hazard ([perception.ts](../src/sim/perception.ts) l. 232–243), and the first male whose roll fires leads.
  - The lead score in [candidates.ts](../src/sim/candidates.ts) l. 642 is `patrolLeadScore + 0.15·(males − 3) + 0.3·boldness + 0.1 if alpha`.
- **Evidence.** No study found records the identity, rank or success of patrol initiators.
  - Nearest evidence:
    - Gombe "impact patrollers": 3 males whose presence during a periphery visit raised the odds of a patrol 2.1–4.1× (Gilby et al. 2013). Two of them later became alpha.
    - An older Gombe description has one male usually at the front of patrols (Goodall 1986, as quoted by Gilby et al. 2013; not read).
  - Rank:
    - Ngogo: patrol frequency and willingness to join were unrelated to rank, and the long-time alpha refrained 40% of the time (Watts & Mitani 2001).
    - Gombe: alphas did not participate more (75% vs 70%, p = 0.32; Massaro et al. 2022).
    - Ngogo 1996–2015: rank predicted *participation* (+0.28; Langergraber et al. 2017).
  - Participation tracks hunting participation (r = 0.66 Ngogo; β = 1.83 Gombe) and gregariousness.
  - General travel (Budongo): vocal initiators recruit better and allies follow (Gruber & Zuberbühler 2013). Patrols are silent, so this transfers only as "followers are bond partners". The sim already has that (`bond(c, leader)·0.3` in the join score).
- **Recommendation.**
  - Remove the alpha lead bonus (0.1 → 0). Two of three direct tests find no rank effect on patrolling.
  - Carry initiation on a stable per-male propensity: boldness today, design. If the sim has a hunting propensity, correlate the two.
  - Keep the "weakly evidenced" label in docs/simulation.md.
- **Confidence:** low-to-moderate.

## 3. Other discrepancies found

1. **Patrols are capped at 2.5 h** (`patrolMaxH`, [execution.ts](../src/sim/execution.ts) l. 348; design).
   - Field durations:
     - Ngogo: 2.13 ± 1.01 h (N = 72), up to ~6 h (Mitani & Watts 2005).
     - Ngogo: 134 min, range 15–348 (Amsler 2010; numbers from the realism pass).
     - Gombe: median 88.5 min, range 3–595 (Massaro et al. 2022).
   - If durations were roughly normal, the cap would cut about a third of Ngogo patrols short (derived).
   - Recommend ≥ 6 h in the field profile. The compressed profile may keep a scaled stand-in, labelled as such.
   - Confidence: moderate.
2. **Male count definition.**
   - The source's odds ratio is per male in the day's maximum party, counting adults *and adolescents* (Mitani & Watts 2005). Ngogo males patrol regularly from age 13 (Langergraber et al. 2017).
   - The sim counts adult males aged ≥ 15 in view (`isAdultMale`, [hierarchy.ts](../src/sim/hierarchy.ts) l. 16).
   - Count males aged ≥ 13 in the hazard, or label the difference.
   - Confidence: moderate; low impact.
3. **Oestrous females are absent from the hazard.** This is consistent with Mitani & Watts 2005 (no effect). No change.
4. **P4a presentation, final tags.**
   - Single file, silence, listening stops, and vigilance until well back toward the centre: **[H]** (Watts & Mitani 2001; Mitani & Watts 2005; Gilby et al. 2013 and Massaro et al. 2022 define patrols by cautious, looking-and-listening travel).
   - The release display on return is supported qualitatively ("sometimes"; Watts & Mitani 2001). The 0.5 and 1.0 probabilities stay design.
   - Edge and return speeds: no source checked. They stay design.
   - Wilson et al. 2007 found calls at the periphery are not uniformly suppressed. That applies to periphery visits in general, not patrols.
